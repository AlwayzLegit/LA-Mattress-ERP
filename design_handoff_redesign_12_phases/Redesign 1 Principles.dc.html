LA MATTRESS ERP · REDESIGN

Phase 1 — Principles and the three-second test

Text only. Nothing here is drawn; everything after this is judged against it.
1a
PRINCIPLES SHEET · ONE PAGE

A register, not a website.
Six rules every screen is measured against.

1 · Three seconds

Every screen has one first question, written on its artboard. The answer is the largest thing on the screen, above the fold, with no click. If two things compete for that spot, one of them leaves.

2 · One obvious path

Strong defaults over choice. The register opens on a new sale. Add Product opens sourcing from the warehouse. Take-with pulls from the selling store. The user can override anything, but never has to decide anything to finish the common case.

3 · Numbers are the interface

Money, quantities and counts set in a tabular display face, aligned right, same width every row. A number without its baseline (same day last week, capacity, total) is decoration and is removed.

4 · State is never colour alone

Six statuses. Each has a colour, a glyph and a word, always together. A grey pill that says "open" next to a grey pill that says "draft" is a bug.

5 · Reachable end to end

Every row is a link. Every dialog traps focus and closes on Escape. Every field has a visible label. A salesperson can write, pay and print a sale without touching the mouse. Shortcuts show the key for the machine they are on.

6 · Nothing half-explained

No bare "Loading…". No "ATP" without a definition. No "showing first 100" without the denominator. If a state exists, it has words, and the words say what to do next.

SALESPERSON · AT THE REGISTER
"What do I need to finish this sale?"

Customer in front of them. Two minutes. They never read the screen; they scan for the next empty thing and the total.

STORE MANAGER · ONE STORE
"What in my store is about to go wrong today?"

Sees only their store. Wants the queue, the risk, who sold what, and whether the drawer closed clean.

DISPATCH · WAREHOUSE
"How full is each day, and what is not ready to load?"

Fifteen stops is a soft cap. Drivers work from paper. Anything not fully reserved is a phone call before the truck leaves.

OWNER · MORNING COFFEE
"Did we write more than last week, and where did it come from?"

One number first, then by store, then by associate, then what the 10pm close flagged. Everything else is a click away.

1b
Design notes — how the sheet was argued

Forstall opened by asking for a seventh rule: "ship the default". His case was that the audit's worst findings (the loading screen, the picker with no focus trap, the grey pills) are not taste problems, they are things nobody decided. He wanted a rule that says every state and every default is somebody's explicit choice. Ive pushed back that a seventh rule dilutes six; that "one obvious path" already says it. The compromise is rule 2's second sentence, which names three defaults from the inventory-source handoff so the rule cannot be read as abstract.

Ive wanted rule 3 to say "numbers are the only decoration". Matas objected that motion is also legitimate when it explains a state change (a line sliding in when added, a total re-counting), and that "only" would be used later to kill the physics that makes direct manipulation feel trustworthy. Settled: rule 3 stays about numbers; motion gets its own line in Phase 2 under the token sheet, scoped to state changes and reduced-motion fallbacks.

Chaudhri argued that the manager's first question is not "what is about to go wrong" but "how are we doing", because the current dashboard leads with sales. Jobs settled it by asking what the manager does after reading the screen: they call a customer or they move stock. Sales-so-far causes no action at 9am; a delivery with a short line does. Managers lead with exceptions and get the numbers second. The owner is the reverse, because the owner's action is a conversation with a store manager, and that conversation starts with a number.

Jobs killed two candidate rules: "consistency" (a means, not a principle — it falls out of one token set) and "delight" (nobody at a register wants to be delighted; they want to be done). Later: the brief locks a left sidebar. The team's better idea — a top strip with the store context and four destinations, sidebar gone — is recorded here and not pursued.

What we removed
  • A "consistency" principle (achieved by tokens, not by stating it).
  • A "delight" principle.
  • Role personas with photos and names — the brief forbids placeholder avatars and the screens do not need them; roles are described by their first question instead.
  • A separate "warehouse" and "dispatch" role. They share a screen set and a first question; splitting them doubled the matrix without changing a decision.

How the count was argued

Forstall wanted Orders above Products: "the register opens Orders every time a customer calls back." Chaudhri pointed out the salesperson opens the product picker inside New Sale four times per sale and the browser itself perhaps twice a day; the picker is counted under New Sale. Products (the browser) earns rank 4 not on opens but because the cross-store question — "who has this anywhere" — is a spec requirement the audit found unanswered. Jobs: rank by frequency, then break ties by how badly the current screen fails its first question.
